Embracing Chaos: Lessons From Three Semesters of Capstone Projects

Created on December 08, 2025

2025   ·   capstone,   computer   sience   education   ·   non-technical

When I designed this capstone for the first time in Fall 2024, the guiding principle was simple: give students as much chance to experience challenges of software engineering in the real world as much as possible. This means to either bringing in projects that either come from real clients’ demands or are much larger in scope compraing to typical course projects. Of course, this comes with risks. In an attempt to provide scaffolding for students, I have tried, in theory, to manage and control the inherent uncertainties in these projects. Three semesters later, I have learned something humbling: as the course matures, the level of uncontrollable chaos grows with it. And paradoxically, it is inside this chaos that the students grow the most. Their final projects stand as evidence of their emerging professional maturity.

In this essay, I want to trace how I have come to see chaos not as a defect to eliminate but as a necessary condition for learning. I will explore how this instability shapes individiual student experiences, as revealed through their final reflections across Fall 2024, Spring 2025, and Fall 2025. And I want to end with an important question I am strill struggle with myself: How do we sustain and control this chaos at just enough to push students forward but not enough to push them over the edge?

Fall 2024: Foundational Chaos (Walk in Uncertainty)

We start our first Capstone semester with small ambitions. Through the Career Development Center and alumni, we were able to receive one project from a business, two from non-profit organizations, and two from faculty. All projects differ from one another in scope, scale, and clients’ technical ability. Students suddenly found themselves responsible not only for writing code but also for imagining what the client meant when they described what they wanted. Across all reflections, several themes emerged with near-perfect consistency:

1. Learning to Translate Ambiguity Into Requirements

Students discovered very quickly that community organizations (or clients without technical background) do not speak in technical terms. Requirements did not come nicely written as typical course assignments. Only through long and repeated conversations with the clients that students learned:

  • How to extract requirements from non-technical conversations.
  • How to repeatedly clarify and confirm understanding, sometimes through extensive rephrasing.
  • How to negotiate gently when clients requested impossible or unclear features
  • How to keep stakeholders updated without overwhelming them

This was their first experience learning that engineering work often begins long before code.

2. Technical Learning by Immersion

For students working with highly technical clients, they repeatedly emphasized how much new technology they had to lear quickly. Examples include ReactJS and TypeScript, frontend–backend integration via OpenAPI or custom APIs, UI/UX design standards and consistency, basic cloud/web hosting concepts, and Git workflows (branching, merging, collaborative version control).

However, a common concluding theme of all these technical learnings was the meta skill: The most important thing I learned was how to learn new technical skills. Students realized that unfamiliar tools were not obstacles but inevitabilities. The reflections consistently described a shift from intimidation to capability: once students realized they could learn one unfamiliar tool, learning the next became less frightening.

3. Real Team Collaboration

Adapting from Cornel University’s Software Engineering course, I have arrange teams to have 4 to 6 students, and the clients were encouraged to scale their projects accordingly. In this setting, students faced genuine interdependence. Their contributions weren’t isolated homework assignments but interconnected components of a real system. There were still cases of slacking off, which were pointed out in students’ reflection. At the same time, almost all reflections recornized the importance of distributing responsibilities based on skill, interest, or necessity and maintaining internal accountability. Many noted that teamwork required more communication than expected, including communication they assumed would “just happen.”

4. Exposure to Professional Expectations

Several reflections mention the sobering experience of realizing that their clients expect progress even when students feel stuck. They recognize the importance of timely and professional communication and the needs for actively reaching out to clients. Even for clients who were non-technical, the responsibility placed on the students felt very real.

5. The First Glimpse of Chaos

In this first semester, there were definitely uncertainties such as non-technical clients with organizational and financial constraints (regarding technical solutions), delivery presssures, and scope adjustments, integration challenges, and conflicting team schedules. What stands out is that, despite everything, students rarely complained about the chaos. I suspected that they have come to embraced it as part of the learning experience. This was demonstrated in their reflections, as many students wrote that they came into the semester unsure if they could build something for a real organization. By the end, the successful delivery of even a modest product fundamentally changed their self-perception.

Spring 2025

If Fall 2024 was the semester of foundational chaos, then Spring 2025 was the semester where chaos became structured, ambitious, and multidimensional. With three business clients and four student-defined projects, this cohort faced more variety, more pressure, and far more technical depth than the first. The students’ reflections reveal a set of powerful interconnected learnings:

1. Ambition Scales Faster Than Stability

The four self-defined projects reflected unrestrained ambition. Students designed ideas involving machine learning pipelines, augmented reality environments, and full-stack tools that rivaled small startup MVPs.

But ambition came with consequences:

Three out of the four student-defined projects ultimately collapsed or stalled

Students discovered the hard truth of scoping, feasibility, and time realism

They learned to revise expectations, redesign components, or abandon pathologically complex directions

Yet these failures were educational, even formative. Students saw firsthand that:

“Just because it’s exciting doesn’t mean it’s possible within 14 weeks.”

This was the semester when students learned to become architects, not just implementers.

  1. Dramatically Expanded Technical Depth

Compared to Fall 2024, Spring 2025’s technical challenges escalated rapidly. Students wrote about learning:

Machine learning (training, models, PyTorch, pipelines)

Augmented reality development tools and integration challenges

Cloud systems (AWS IAM, policies, dashboards, monitoring)

Microservices and API integration

Containerization and deployment pipelines

This forced students to confront advanced concepts normally spread across upper-level electives. Many admitted being intimidated at first, but they repeatedly described the same turning point:

“Once I realized it was just math, statistics, and code, it became manageable.”

This wasn’t just technical learning—it was identity formation.

  1. Professional Communication as a Discipline, Not a Courtesy

Business clients in Spring 2025 were more structured and more demanding. Students had to:

Provide consistent, timely progress reports

Seek meetings rather than wait for clients to initiate

Handle unresponsiveness professionally, without losing momentum

Negotiate shifting requirements

Push for clarity when specifications were incomplete

Several students noted that the discomfort of nudging a client—especially a senior professional—was a major learning experience. They began to understand that communication in engineering isn’t optional; it’s a core competency.

For many, this was the first semester they felt the weight of professionalism.

  1. Team Organization and Distributed Roles

Reflections consistently mentioned:

Weekly sprint meetings

Clear assignment of roles

Delegation of responsibilities

Cross-component integration challenges

One-on-one coordination when two subsystems needed to talk to each other

Students learned that successful teams don’t just divide work—they synchronize work.

The internal leadership that emerged in several teams was noteworthy. Students independently began adopting industry practices because the chaos demanded it.

  1. Pivoting as a Survival Skill

A critical theme across reflections:

Some teams learned that their early assumptions were wrong

Others learned that vendor access or data permissions wouldn’t materialize

Still others realized their original architecture was fundamentally flawed

But instead of succumbing to inertia, students learned to pivot—to redefine the project, re-architect the system, or build only the most essential features.

This was the semester where students learned that engineering isn’t linear. The ability to change direction gracefully, strategically, and without drama is a hallmark of maturity.

  1. A Surprising Model Emerges: Some Projects Die, One Becomes Real

Only one self-defined project survived and continued beyond the semester. Students reflected on why:

Tight scope

Clear value proposition

Steady progress

Strong internal communication

Feasible technical stack

Its survival taught students a powerful lesson: success is built, not imagined.

The other three projects taught their own lesson: failure is not waste—it is clarity.

Fall 2025

Fall 2025 was the semester where chaos stopped pretending to be a side effect and instead became the defining structure of the course. The class took on four business clients and two self-defined projects, both of which became unexpectedly significant—one evolving into a startup prototype, the other aiming for long-term departmental use.

This semester’s reflections were deeper, more introspective, and more candid than any prior cohort.

  1. Crisis as Catalyst: Learning Through Client Failure

Two teams experienced complete client collapse—disappearing contacts, unclear requirements, or access that never materialized. This forced students into a radical learning moment:

They rebuilt entire projects from scratch in seven weeks.

Students learned:

How to scope a new project under severe time pressure

How to divide responsibilities aggressively

How to set realistic but ambitious goals

How to keep morale intact despite disappointment

This was not simulated chaos. It was genuine professional adversity, and the students met it.

Their reflections repeatedly describe these crises as:

“Humbling,” “Motivating,” “Transformational,” “The moment everything clicked.”

  1. Technical Systems Across Architectures and Environments

Compared to previous semesters, the technical complexity jumped again:

Cross-architecture development (ARM vs AMD)

Integration with edge devices and drones

Performance optimization under constrained environments

Real-time pipelines, latency considerations, model runtime limits

Database migrations, API redesigns, and UI/UX refinements

Containerization strategies that needed to be portable

Students were no longer just learning technologies—they were learning systems thinking.

They discovered that:

Trade-offs are unavoidable

Architecture is negotiation

Performance matters

Constraints are not excuses

Fall 2025 was the semester of engineering judgment.

  1. Leadership and Delegation Under High Pressure

In almost every reflection, someone mentioned the turning point: the moment they realized the team would fail without clear delegation.

Students learned:

Leadership is not charisma—it is responsibility

Delegation is not optional when the clock is ticking

Even strong personalities can become effective collaborators

Accountability is easier when tasks are explicit, not implicit

Your now-legendary “delegate or fail” meeting appears in multiple reflections as a major inflection point.

  1. The Emergence of Entrepreneurs

One self-defined project didn’t just survive—it became the foundation of a small company. Students worked with:

The campus business development center

Legal resources

Market research frameworks

Feasibility assessments

Their reflections show a remarkable transition: They stopped thinking like project builders and started thinking like founders.

The other self-defined project produced a tool intended for future students—evidence of long-term thinking and responsibility toward the academic community.

This was the first semester where entrepreneurship emerged organically.

  1. Social and Professional Maturity

Students candidly documented learning:

To communicate expectations internally rather than assume mutual understanding

To show up early and prepared for presentations

To navigate interpersonal tension and uneven responsibility

To maintain morale during periods of uncertainty

To recognize when personal weaknesses affect team performance

The emotional honesty in this cohort’s reflections was striking—they didn’t just describe what they did; they described who they became while doing it.

The ability to rebuild when everything collapses

Fall 2025 is when students stopped learning how to do engineering and started learning how engineers survive, adapt, and lead in unstable environments.

III. Conclusion: Three Semesters of Chaos, and the Road Ahead

Compared to Drexel’s co-op model or Northeastern’s internship pipeline, my capstone sits in an interesting middle ground. Co-ops offer long-term immersion; internships provide professional context. What we provide is compressed reality—a high-expectation, high-variation, rapid-learning environment where students experience the messy parts of engineering that even co-ops sometimes shield them from.

Our students don’t just work in industry; they practice becoming professionals under conditions that resemble year two of a real job, not week two of an internship.

If there is a call to action, it is this:

Regional businesses and community organizations should not underestimate the talent growing in their own backyard. Partnering with universities—especially state universities—creates an ecosystem where students thrive, local industries gain innovators, and communities benefit from fresh technical capacity.

The past three semesters proved that when students are given real responsibility, real risk, and real support, they don’t just rise to the challenge—they redefine what a capstone can be.
