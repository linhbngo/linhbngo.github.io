Raising the Bar Within the Same Four Years

Created on November 25, 2026

2026   ·   cs-education,   artificial-intelligence   ·   non-technical

I talked about how software jobs are moving to non-traditional places in May 2025, entry-level positions in the future in August 2025. Now, only a bit over a year since then, the impact of AI in science and technology has been rapid and significant, while longer-term consequences on society remain uncertain. Most notably, within the past week, AI-led breakthroughs in mathematics claimed to have proved the Riemann Hypothesis and the Navier-Stokes equation. Within the last two days, in Computer Science, one AI-supported work claimed to prove VP != VNP, and the other announced that the matching lower bound for the matrix multiplication exponent is exactly $(\omega = 9/4) ((2.25))$. These discoveries have created a wave of excitement, skepticism, and concerns in and for the fields of Mathematics and Computer Science. While these results are still undergoing various stages of verification, the sheer frequency with which potential breakthroughs are being announced raises an interesting question: Can our capacity to understand and make use of scientific discoveries keep pace with our increasing capacity to produce them? Of course, another popular doomsday theme also popped up almost immediately questioning the values of the bachelor degree in these fields and perhaps in general sciences (Chemistry, Physics, and Biology).

For Computer Science (CS), a glance at LinkedIn and other academic job boards shows hiring in CS faculty remain pretty much highly active for R1 institutions (R1: very high research activities). While this does not automatically translate into positive CS employment prospects for undergraduate CS majors, it suggests that research-intensive universities continue to see considerable values in the discipline. For those of us at non-R1 institutions, where undergraduate education and career preparation are central to our mission, the question is somewhat different. If AI substantially accelerates the production of scientific knowledge, there may also be a growing need for people who can understand, evaluate, and translate this knowledge into practical applications. Should we then reconsider what and how we teach our undergraduate CS students, not because the science itself becomes less relevant, but because the nature of the work that most of our graduates will be doing might be fundamentally changed?

Computer Science in the Pre-AI Era

CS occupies a somewhat interesting position among scientific and technological disciplines. Over the past several decades, CS has taken on several roles simultaneously. First, CS is a scientific discipline studying the nature and principles of computation. This is where we have the core courses such as discrete mathematics, fundamentals of computer science, data structures and algorithms. Next, CS is an engineering discipline concerned with the design and construction of computational system and infrastructures. Here, we have networking, computer security, cloud computing, high performance computing, etc. Perhaps the more popular role, to the typical non-CS folks, of the field is a professional discipline preparing students for careers in software development, with courses such as software engineering, UI/UX, testing, and web application development. Then, CS is also an enabling discipline providing computational capabilities to other scientific and professional fields with courses with computational-X or X-computing themes (e.g., statistical computing or scientific computing).

While these roles are closely related, and the later three are generally presumed to be built upon the core courses of the first roles, in practice, they do not necessarily require the same set of knowledge and skills. And yet, the rapid expansion of the software industry over the past several decades has allowed undergraduate CS programs to accommodate these different roles within a single degree, BS in Computer Science. The demand for software developers was sufficiently large that students could pursue a relatively general CS education and still have reasonable employment prospects upon graduation.

This is somewhat different from other scientific disciplines. Even before AI, we do not necessarily expect someone with a bachelor’s degree in Physics to become physicist, or someone with a bachelor’s degree in Biology to become biologist. Physics and Biology students have many other potential paths and becoming a physicist or biologist immediately after graduation is not the default mode. Yet, for a long time, becoming a software developer has been an almost default career expectation for someone graduating with a CS degree. This expectation has also influenced how we design our CS curricula, how we recruit prospective students, and how students themselves perceive the discipline.

In my earlier essays, I made the case that there is still plenty of software development work, although they are no longer concentrated at typical pure software development companies. Rather, smaller businesses, non-profits, and local government entities are undergoing their own transition to catch up with the rapid development of modern software and infrastructures. This has been anecdotally observed in our Software Engineering Capstone’s external clients. I still believe this to be the case. However, given the rapid pace of AI adoption, I am beginning to question whether the growth in demand for software will necessarily translate into a continued demand for software developers. If it does not, can we still rely on software development as the primary employment justification for a relatively general undergraduate CS degree?

Computer Science in the AI Era

This is a big topic, and every CS faculty member and their mothers will have their different perspectives. I want to look at this issue from a pragmatic view of a non-R1 institution whose primary mission is to prepare our students for a career in some fields, not necessarily pure computing, but would let them leverage what they have learned (and paid to learn) during their four years of college.

Let us start with a thought exercise, which was motivated from a conversation I had with one of my students. They were advertising themselves for web development services, but they were worried that AI’s vibe coding trend is enabling many people to create their own websites for their businesses without having to hire someone from outside anymore. What should a web developer of the future do then, to remain relevant?

Suppose I own a small bakery and I want a website done. Assuming I know nothing about CS and web development, this is a task that I would probably hire someone for. However, with AI and vibe coding, I can probably create a reasonably good looking, attractive even, website to advertise my products. The website drives more customers to my store, and they start asking for the ability to online order. I could pay a fee for Door Dash, Uber Eat, or yet another food delivery company, or I can try to build this into my website. Now I need to know how to integrate online orders with my existing point-of-sale system, maybe manage my inventory based on these orders, or more ambitiously, use historical sales data to help plan my production schedule. Of course, with all of these fancy technologies, a simple web-hosting might not be adequate, and I would need to explore AWS-like spaces.

In theory, AI can help me implement these functionalities as well, assuming I know what specific prompts to create, how to evaluate what AI has produced, and how to work together with AI in problem solving when things stop working. As a bakery owner, I likely don’t have time to learn any of these (unless I am a CS graduate turned baker), and I probably will now resort to hiring someone.

One key issue here though, I am still expecting the cost of hiring to be close to that of an entry-level person, since I only have the budget of a small bakery, even though the work is now on par with the infrastructure of a medium business. This is perhaps a reasonable example of AI raising the expectation of entry-level computing professionals. The financial barrier to developing increasingly sophisticated software system is getting lower, while the expectations regarding what an individual computing professional can accomplish within that financial constraint are getting higher.

There are plot holes in the above examples. The baker might not need custom software at all and they just need to pay for a consultant service that help them set up with existing services. Furthermore, entry-level budget does not mean entry-level hiring, and an independent experienced consultant with AI can complete the proposed work. However, even with these plot holes, it seems that this scenario does not bode well for a typical graduating CS student. To go for that entry-level compensation, they are now expected to perform at the level of experienced professionals. While we as educators have little control over the changing dynamics of the employment market, we do have some control over how we prepare our students for it. How, then, do we help our students meet these new technical expectations within the same four years of undergraduate education?

Raising the Bar Within the Same Four Years

It is always interesting looking at old pictures and recordings of athletes. With very few exceptions (Michael Jordan!), most modern professional athletes would probably smoke their historical counterparts in competitions. This is not necessarily because humans have become inherently more athletic, but because we have become much better at training athletes. Even within CS, looking at what students need to know now and comparing with what I had to learn in college, it is obvious that the bar has been rising. We have been accommodating this rising bar through gradual changes to our curricula and teaching methodologies. However, given the rapid advances in AI, I suspect that the rate at which this bar is rising may soon outpace the rate at which we traditionally adapt our undergraduate education.

One interesting aspect of athletic development is that we have not necessarily given athletes more years to prepare for professional competition. Rather, we have become more effective at utilizing those years. Similarly, we cannot reasonably expect our students to spend another two or three years in college every time there is a significant technological advancement. Nor can we keep adding new courses to an undergraduate curriculum with a relatively fixed number of credits.

In fact, let’s go back to our imaginary bakery. All topics related to the listed requirements are already covered in our typical general curriculum. Roughly speaking, website and online ordering is from web application development. Point-of-sale and other service integration are from topics in software engineering. Inventory management is certainly from database system. Production forecasting is a combination of web application development and machine learning/data analytic. Hosting and deployment can come from networking or cloud computing. The development of payment methods and customer information will need contents from computer security. Finally, trouble shooting and maintenance of the entire she-bang can come from knowledge in computer systems and operating systems.

The biggest issue here though, is that none of these courses would specifically talk about the infrastructure for a bakery, nor should they! In fact, the contents from these courses may appear seemingly unrelated to students at times. When students work on a problem in a database course, they already know that they are expected to use a database. Our imaginary baker, on the other hand, might simply complain about having to throw away too many unsold croissants every evening. It is up to the computing professional to figure out which pieces of their technical knowledge, if any, are relevant to this problem.

Traditionally, we have been able to rely, at least partially, on the workplace to help our graduates develop this ability. Students, when joining the workforce either permanently through jobs or temporarily through relevant internships, will begin accumulating experience and gradually learn to combine and transfer knowledge acquired from different courses to unfamiliar situations. But what if students are required to already know how to transfer learn right out of the gate? Going back to my analogy at the beginning of the section, an athlete does not spend four years learning and practicing how to pass, shoot, dribble, and defend it in separate courses, only to play an actual basketball game during their final semester.

Once again, it is not that CS has not already been implementing this aspect. Experiential learning has been frequently promoted, and in most cases, manifested in the setting of capstone experience. But perhaps it is not early enough and extensive enough. Perhaps transfer of learning, including more hands-on and project-based work, should be an explicit and continuously practiced learning objective throughout the CS curriculum. More importantly, these activities should increasingly require students to recognize, select, and combine relevant knowledge from their previous learning experiences, rather than simply applying the specific knowledge being taught in a particular course.That would be one way to help our students keep pace with the rapidly rising barrier of entry into the workforce in this AI-era, without spending more time.

Conclusion: A Hypothetical Example

Let’s take my server-side web application development course as an example. At the moment, the theme of the project is for students to develop a web application, which reinforces the knowledge acquired in that course. At the same time, I already have students using containers for their computing environment setup. A modification to promote transfer of learning could be to set limits to the capacity (CPU and memory) of the server container, require the applications to support a large number of simultaneous users, ensure transmission of sensitive information, and maintain data consistency across concurrent transactions. In addressing these requirements, students would need to draw upon knowledge from operating systems (resource management and concurrency), computer networking and security (HTTPS/TLS), and database systems (normalization, constraints, and transaction management). It is still the same project theme, but students are now expected to recognize and bring together relevant knowledge from several other courses.

These approaches are not without significant challenges. They increase the complexity of designing, supervising, and assessing student work. At non-R1 institutions, faculty teaching loads and available resources are already significant constraints. In this case, is it possible that AI, the same technology that raises the expectations for our graduates, can also enable us to provide the extensive learning experience necessary to meet those expectations.
