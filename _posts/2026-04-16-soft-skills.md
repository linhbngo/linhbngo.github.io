The calls for embedding soft skills in colleges have now become routine. Students, we are told, 
should graduate able to communicate, collaborate, adapt, think strategically, manage conflict, 
and work well with others. Yet, in a similar routine, hiring managers and recruiters from industry 
would keep coming back and raise the issue about how new employees are often lacking in these areas.

It would be unfair to say that colleges do not teach these things at all. In fact, we do it quite 
extensively. Students learn the fundamentals of communication in writing and speaking courses. They 
practice presenting in technical courses. They are assigned group work where they are expected to 
collaborate. They complete larger projects that require some planning, some adaptation, some division 
of labor, and some strategic thinking. They learn the form of a professional email. They learn that 
meetings should have structure. On paper, much of the machinery is already there. What is still missing?

In this essay, I want to present my thoughts on three topics:

- Why the current components of soft skills are not fully internalized by many students?
- How capstone courses could be the exact place where this internalization can happen?
- Is it possible for this to happen earlier?

## Weakness of consequences

I want to begin this section with my anecdotal observation on a Software Engineering Capstone (`CSC402`) 
course that I have taught every semester since Fall 2024. At the beginning, the motivation for this 
course was simply to provide students with an experiential learning experience by working on projects 
provided by external clients. As this is a course for seniors that are close to graduation, they are 
expected to come in with *adequate* technical preparation through previous electives. This course is 
not meant to be a gate keeper but to serve as a personal measure stick: to see how much everyone can 
accomplish before stepping out to the real world. At the end of the semester, each group will present 
their work, not in class, but in a public setting, in front of invited guests (deans, parents, clients, 
other students, etc) and on a large stage.  

The course has been a resounding success, as per both students' interests (the offering session is full 
only after one or two days of registration, with many stay on the wait list) and their post-course 
evaluation. One thing that surprised me in their post-course evaluation is the fact that they praised 
how the course has made them become better in team work and communication and other soft-skill-related 
aspects. I asked myself, did they not learn communication skills earlier? After talking with other students 
that are outside of CSC402, they all mentioned how specific communication techniques are taught in 
their speaking classes. The question is then how did CSC 402 make its students become better at soft skills?

As I stated at the beginning, students learn communication and other soft skills in pieces across different 
courses. In these settings, they practice the skills where the stakes remain mostly academic. 
A presentation is often still just a presentation for a grade. Group work is often just group work in the 
peculiar ecology of school, where one competent student carries the project, or a cluster of close friends 
works smoothly because friendship covers for poor process, or everyone learns the delicate art of pretending 
that equal contribution occurred. Adaptation happens, but usually only enough to satisfy the assignment. 
Strategic thinking appears, but often in rubric-shaped form. Conflict management is less about managing 
conflict than about avoiding it. Everyone is nice and everyone is agreeable. Problems are swept under the 
rug because the semester is short and the grade matters more than the health of the collaboration. In that environment, students are not learning nothing. They are learning early forms of soft skills. However,
 early forms are not the same as internalized habits.

## What happens in 402, stays in 402

While grades in CSC402 are not the primary emphasis, the non-grade consequences seems to be more significant 
for students. Over past 3 semesters (Fall 24, Spring/Fall 25) since the course began being offered, all groups 
have been successful in meeting external clients' requirements and creating deliverable end-products. Perhaps, 
the fear of not meeting a client's demands in this class is being translated as not being successful in the 
real world. Or, perhaps, the internal drive to be as good as or even better than the other teams in front 
of one's family and other strangers is a lot more motivating than doing just enough to not fail the class. 

This is probably the key to my initial second question about why capstone matters. Not because it suddenly 
introduces soft skills from nowhere, but because it changes the conditions under which those skills operate. 
A capstone with an external client and a public showcase does something that much of the earlier curriculum 
does not. It makes these skills consequential. It puts students in a setting where communication and 
collaboration are no longer just performance for evaluation, but tools that actually shape the work. In this 
setting, lack of collaboration makes the work impossible, miscommunications are expensive and carry potential 
impact down the road. In general, it makes professional habits, or the lack of them, difficult to hide.

A student may learn in a communication course how to write a polite and professional summary email after 
a meeting. In class, that can easily feel like busy work, the sort of exercise that exists mainly 
to prove that someone somewhere values professionalism. Then the same student enters a capstone project, 
leaves a meeting with a client, does not send a summary, and a week later discovers that everyone remembers 
the conversation differently. As the client adds features as if they had always been discussed, the 
team realizes that a missing email was not missing paperwork but missing control and leverage for pushing 
back. Suddenly, the summary email is no longer classroom etiquette but a critical component in every team's 
workflow.

That pattern repeats elsewhere. Students learn to present earlier in the curriculum, but in capstone they discover that presenting technical work to a client is not the same as explaining a concept to an instructor who already knows what they mean. They learn to collaborate in earlier courses, but in capstone they discover that collaboration is not just dividing tasks and recombining slides the night before the deadline. It is documenting decisions, managing uneven effort, reading the room, deciding when to push back, and figuring out how to disagree without damaging trust. They may have learned to adapt in other projects, but in capstone they discover that adaptation is not merely changing plans to keep a grade intact. It is responding to shifting expectations, new information, and external pressures without losing coherence.

In short, capstone does not merely teach soft skills. It reveals whether earlier exposure to those skills has actually taken root.

That, I think, is the more interesting argument. It is too easy to say that colleges ignore these capacities and that capstone heroically repairs the damage. The truth is messier. Colleges often do teach pieces of these skills. They simply teach them in forms too weak, too isolated, or too low-stakes to make them stick. Students rehearse them without fully needing them. They perform them without internalizing them. They learn the vocabulary of professionalism before they understand its necessity.

## Is It Late?

This should not be surprising. The same is true of technical knowledge. Students can memorize syntax without learning how to debug. They can follow a lab manual without learning how to diagnose failure. They can finish assignments without learning how to design under uncertainty. We generally accept that technical skills mature through use, friction, revision, and consequence. Yet with soft skills, colleges often act as if one or two communication courses plus a generous sprinkling of group projects should do the trick. The hope seems to be that these fragments will somehow assemble themselves into professional judgment by graduation. Sometimes they do. Often they do not. Hope, as usual, is not a design strategy.

What capstone suggests is not that soft skills belong only at the end. It suggests the opposite. If these capacities become real only when the stakes become real, then one has to ask whether capstone is doing too much too late.

That is the question that keeps pressing at the edge of this discussion. Is capstone too late and too short? By the time students arrive there, they are already near graduation. The semester is brief. The habits are half-formed. The stakes rise all at once. For some students, this works well. Pressure clarifies. They grow quickly. They see the point. For others, capstone becomes the first time they are truly asked to internalize forms of communication, collaboration, and professional accountability that the rest of the curriculum mostly introduced but did not insist upon. That is a lot to demand from one course near the end.

It may be that capstone is less a solution than a diagnostic device. It shows what the curriculum has prepared students to do, and what it has quietly assumed would emerge on its own.

If that is right, then the educational question is not whether capstone is valuable. It clearly is. The question is whether some of its consequential force can be brought earlier into the curriculum, in smaller and more deliberate ways. Not by pretending every course is a capstone. That would be exhausting and slightly absurd. Students do not need a full external-client ordeal every semester. But they may need more courses where communication is tied to live decision-making rather than end-of-unit performance. They may need projects where written summaries matter because later work depends on them. They may need presentations to mixed audiences, not only expert instructors. They may need structured disagreement rather than polite avoidance. They may need team processes that expose unequal labor instead of quietly rewarding it. They may need reflection not just on what they built, but on how the group functioned, how scope changed, how assumptions shifted, and how communication either stabilized or destabilized the work.

That kind of design would not magically produce maturity, and it would not replace the special role of capstone. But it could introduce consequence earlier. It could make these skills less sudden. It could let students practice them before the final act, when there is still time to revise habits rather than merely expose them.

This, of course, puts pressure back where it belongs: on faculty and course design. Colleges can announce that soft skills matter. That part is easy. They can require speech courses and writing courses. Those courses should indeed teach fundamentals, and they matter more than some disciplinary faculty like to admit. But fundamentals do not become professional habits on their own. Someone has to build situations in which they matter. Someone has to insist that students do more than gesture at collaboration, more than perform confidence, more than treat communication as a polite shell around the “real” technical work. In practice, that means faculty in domain courses.

That is not entirely fair, but it is true. Faculty are the ones who make soft skills real or keep them decorative. They decide whether meeting summaries are busywork or part of project control. They decide whether presentations are just polished endings or occasions for actual technical judgment. They decide whether group work is merely assigned or meaningfully structured. They decide whether conflict is ignored in the name of harmony or treated as something students must learn to navigate professionally. Colleges may name the outcomes. Faculty operationalize them.

The difficulty is that not all faculty are equally prepared to do this. Some have deep professional experience and can coach these habits naturally. Others have strong academic backgrounds but less direct experience in the kinds of workplace settings students are entering. Many are already overloaded and are rewarded far more for disciplinary content than for teaching professional judgment. So the challenge here is real. But that does not make the need less real. It only means the institution cannot congratulate itself too quickly for listing soft skills in the catalog while leaving the actual work of teaching them vague, uneven, and late.

## Conclusion

The larger point is simple enough. Students do not fail to learn soft skills because colleges never mention them. They fail to internalize them because the curriculum often teaches them in fragments and practices them under conditions too weak to make them necessary. Capstone, especially with external clients and public showcase, changes that by attaching consequence to what had previously been academic form. It shows that communication, collaboration, adaptability, and conflict management are not accessories to technical education. They are part of what makes technical work hold together in the first place.

If colleges are serious about soft skills, then capstone should not be the first place where those skills become real. It may be the fullest version of that reality, but it should not be the first. The rest of the curriculum should be asking, in smaller and more intentional ways, how to make these capacities consequential earlier. Otherwise we are not really teaching soft skills across college. We are introducing them lightly, postponing their seriousness, and hoping one late course can turn rehearsal into habit. Sometimes it can. That does not mean it should have to.
